## Differential GNSS (DGNSS)

Differential GNSS (DGNSS) enhances positioning accuracy by leveraging real-time communication between a ground-based reference station and a mobile GNSS receiver. These reference stations, situated at precisely known coordinates, continuously monitor GNSS satellite signals and compute the discrepancies between the actual and expected measurements. From this comparison, the reference station generates correction information—specifically, the Pseudorange Correction (PRC) and the Rate of Pseudorange Correction (RRC)—which is broadcast to nearby receivers. These corrections are applied by the receivers to improve the accuracy of their own GNSS measurements. This approach enables near-instantaneous positioning enhancement with relatively low computational complexity, making DGNSS suitable for applications that require real-time performance with moderate accuracy.

However, DGNSS performance can degrade significantly in urban environments. Tall buildings and other man-made structures often cause signal obstructions, leading to multipath propagation where satellite signals reflect off surfaces before reaching the receiver. These reflections introduce significant measurement errors that are difficult to correct using PRC and RRC data. Moreover, the effectiveness of DGNSS depends heavily on the proximity between the reference station and the mobile receiver (rover). As this distance increases, spatially varying errors such as atmospheric delays become more difficult to compensate, thereby reducing positioning accuracy. Terrain and infrastructure constraints also limit the density and distribution of ground reference stations, restricting correction availability in certain regions. Smartphones, with their low-quality GNSS chipsets and embedded antennas, are particularly vulnerable to multipath effects and signal noise, resulting in reduced positioning reliability when using DGNSS in urban settings.

## RTK

Real-Time Kinematic (RTK) positioning provides significantly higher localization accuracy by utilizing carrier-phase measurements in conjunction with a double-difference technique. Unlike DGNSS, which relies on code-based pseudorange data, RTK uses the phase of the carrier signal, which has a much higher frequency. For example, while the GPS L1 C/A code operates at 1.023 MHz, the corresponding carrier frequency is 1575.42 MHz, allowing for finer resolution and improved precision. The double-difference technique involves differencing measurements from the same satellite between two receivers (base and rover), and then differencing measurements from two satellites at a single receiver. This method eliminates common-mode errors such as satellite clock drift and ionospheric delay, making it possible to achieve centimeter-level accuracy.

Despite its accuracy, RTK is challenging to implement on smartphones. It requires continuous and reliable communication with a nearby reference station, typically within 10 to 20 kilometers. Beyond this range, spatial errors accumulate and cannot be fully corrected, resulting in reduced accuracy. In urban areas, maintaining a stable communication link is difficult due to signal blockage and interference from buildings. Additionally, not all smartphone GNSS chipsets support RTK, and even when they do, hardware limitations severely impact performance. Research by Humphreys et al. \\cite{7479707} found that smartphone-grade GNSS antennas can suffer gain losses up to 11 dB, while low-end surveying-grade antennas lose less than 1 dB. This implies that smartphone antennas can capture only around 8\\% of the satellite signal strength, making RTK positioning on mobile devices unreliable without external hardware.

## PPP

Precise Point Positioning (PPP) is a satellite-based technique that achieves high-precision positioning by using precise satellite orbit and clock data. Unlike DGNSS and RTK, PPP does not require a local reference station, making it inherently more scalable and suitable for global applications. It provides globally consistent solutions and is especially useful in remote areas where ground infrastructure is lacking. By applying satellite-based corrections, PPP can achieve centimeter-level accuracy under optimal conditions.

However, PPP has its drawbacks, particularly in smartphone applications. One of the most critical limitations is the long convergence time required to attain high accuracy. In static scenarios, it may take several minutes to hours—and in some studies, up to 24 hours—to achieve centimeter-level precision. This is far longer than the convergence time for DGNSS and RTK, which are typically on the order of seconds to a few minutes. Moreover, smartphones face significant challenges due to their hardware. Lower-quality GNSS components, such as oscillators and antennas, reduce the signal-to-noise ratio and increase the likelihood of multipath errors. This results in frequent loss of carrier-phase tracking and less reliable position estimates. These issues limit the practical use of PPP for real-time, high-accuracy positioning on mobile devices \\cite{wang2021real}. Overall, while PPP is promising in theory, its real-world performance on smartphones is constrained by both time and hardware limitations.

## PPP-RTK

PPP-RTK merges the strengths of PPP and RTK, offering a hybrid approach that delivers both high accuracy and faster convergence. Like PPP, it uses satellite-based precise orbit and clock information, but it augments this data with atmospheric and hardware bias corrections delivered from a regional network. This additional information enables fast ambiguity resolution and centimeter-level accuracy within a few seconds to minutes, even in mobile scenarios. One of PPP-RTK’s key advantages is its reduced reliance on dense local infrastructure. It can function effectively over broader geographic areas with fewer reference stations than required for RTK, making it a more scalable and practical solution for smartphone applications.

Nonetheless, PPP-RTK still faces limitations on smartphones. The accuracy of this technique depends on the quality of the GNSS data, which is affected by the phone's embedded hardware. In a recent study, the root mean square (RMS) of pseudorange and phase residuals for the G04 satellite, when tracked using the Huawei P40 smartphone’s embedded antenna, were 5.56 m and 0.30 cm, respectively. These values were approximately 6.5 and 1.4 times higher than those obtained when using an external antenna, underscoring the negative impact of smartphone-grade antennas. The study further linked this degradation to severe multipath effects introduced by the embedded antenna, as evident from high variation in residuals and signal-to-noise ratios \\cite{10213417}. Additionally, the computational requirements for processing real-time SSR (State Space Representation) corrections on a smartphone remain a challenge, particularly for devices with limited processing power.

## Conclusion

In summary, all four GNSS techniques—DGNSS, RTK, PPP, and PPP-RTK—have shown substantial potential for enhancing positioning accuracy, but their performance on smartphones varies significantly due to hardware and environmental limitations. DGNSS offers moderate improvements with low complexity but suffers in urban areas and over long distances. RTK achieves better accuracy but requires constant connectivity with a nearby base station and is hampered by smartphone hardware. PPP eliminates the need for local infrastructure and is globally consistent, but convergence times and signal quality on mobile devices limit its practicality. PPP-RTK provides a promising balance, combining fast convergence and centimeter accuracy with broader scalability. However, it too is constrained by smartphone-grade hardware and computational demand. Embedded antenna normally cannot provide good strength of signal and chipset in smartphone also limits the performance on algorithms using carrier phase.
